Why my idea doesn't actually work (yet)

October 2026

I bumped into a classic problem in integration architecture. Systems talk to each other through APIs and queues, and when a message fails on its way from one to another, it ends up in a dead letter queue. It sits there, and it doesn't matter much unless you decide to do something about it. Some of those messages are worth reprocessing. Others are only there to make you aware of something. It is worth keeping them as data: analysed together, they can power dashboards and give real insight into how the system behaves.

How much it hurts depends on your line of business. It can be a payment that failed because of an unsupported currency or an invalid card number. It can be an order on your e-commerce site that fails because, when it’s time to deduct the stock at your external warehouse, the item is no longer there.

I thought it was a real pain for support engineers and SREs, and I started thinking about a solution that would make their work easier and more efficient. I’m one of them myself. But above all, it matters to their companies, which may have strict SLAs (service level agreements) on performance or response time.

So I called two friends of mine and explained the idea, and surprisingly they were willing to help me make it real. We spent our evenings on it for a few weeks and ended up with a working tool. It was a good exercise, and this is how it started.

The tool is simply a small control plane for failed messages, bridging the gap between the alert and the action. Monitors say when a queue is filling up or a message has waited too long. The console lets you search those messages, inspect them and quickly repair their payload. You can replay them one at a time or in bulk, and the console draws a map of how your queues connect.

When you build something like this, the next question is whether anyone would pay for it. So how big is the opportunity? It’s easy to start from the big number, and here it belongs to observability, the market for tools that watch your systems. It’s forecast to reach about $14 billion by 2028. Failed messages sit close to it, so it feels like we're standing next to something huge.

But nobody has a budget line called "failed messages". The money has to come from somewhere else: an incident that lost orders, an audit where nobody can say who replayed a payment, or engineers losing a day a week.

Nobody has built a company on this, and that's a warning. When something this obvious doesn't exist, the market is usually too small or the pain isn't worth paying for. The real competitor is doing nothing, or the script someone wrote years ago that still more or less works.

Every customer would ask the same question: why shouldn't we just build it? They could, and it's getting easier. AI helped us build this in a few weeks of evenings. It can do the same for them.

Then there's getting in the door. Onboarding at a medium or large organization is a nightmare, and we know it. If they commit to that, this has to be 10x better than what they do today. And they want fewer tools, not one more.

It might be a good small business, but VCs won't even touch it. I'm not sure where the ceiling is, but I'm quite sure it's too low for them.

There's also a slower threat. A newer kind of platform saves a program's progress at every step, so after a crash the work picks up where it stopped. Retries are built in, so fewer messages get parked at all. If that wins, our problem shrinks on its own.

Now the same evidence, read the other way.

A ceiling only matters if you need to go past it. With no investors to pay back, a small business is fine, and a feature can be a business.

Nobody has built a company on this, but everybody has built the tool. Companies have been writing their own replay tools for years, and none has won. It could mean the script is good enough, but people don't keep rewriting what's good enough.

Monitoring tools tell you the number is high, but nobody is taking action or prioritising it. One team's dead letter queue went unread for days while customers' refunds silently failed. And retrying alone doesn't fix my two examples: the same invalid card fails again, and the warehouse still has no stock. Someone has to decide what happens next, and whether a failed payment comes before a stock update.

That also changes the "one more tool" objection. Next to a script nobody maintains, this replaces something. And the first step can be small: a read-only trial, with no write access on day one.

The market is changing, and not only against us. Machines are getting good at finding the problem, but people don't fully trust them to fix it. That's the same gap between the alert and the action, and somebody still has to own it.

As for those newer platforms, they only help code written from now on. A company with 150 queues won't rewrite a working system to avoid a failure it has learned to live with. And they still can't fix an invalid card.

So far, the only company our tool has worked with is one we invented. A fictional retailer of boats, parts and maintenance, with a back end that really fails. Its dead letter queues fill up because its services fail, not because we typed in fake rows. We can take down a manufacturer's registration portal and watch engine registrations, warranty claims and ERP updates pile up together.

It was useful, but Speedboat is the best case, because we designed it. Its messages carry our own error labels. Real messages won't. They'll be shaped by whatever framework a company uses, and they'll fail in ways nobody planned.

All of this is still reasoning. We stopped before the first conversation with a real customer, so we don't know yet. The idea doesn't fail on the problem. It fails, for now, on proof. That's what the (yet) in the title is for.
